General methodology

This is the public, educational introduction to what this repository does: how it turns long histories of exchange-traded fund (ETF) prices into the backtested strategies and benchmarks the dashboard publishes. It is written for a general reader and stays at the level of concepts. It deliberately avoids configuration detail, the optimisation schema, and the more advanced research methods. Those live in dedicated documents:

  • the configurable strategy formalism and the grid-search schema → GRID_SEARCH.md
  • which strategies use which features → FEATURE_MATRIX.md
  • the advanced / research-driven methods → METHODOLOGY_ADVANCED.md
  • the literature citations → BIBLIOGRAPHY.md

Behaviour is driven entirely by the strategy configuration files in strategies/ and by the asset catalog metadata, never by editing the source code.

1. What the framework does

The repository implements momentum rotation over a curated set of PEA-eligible UCITS ETFs (the funds a French retail investor can hold inside a Plan d'Épargne en Actions). The idea is simple and classic:

  1. Score each fund by how well it has performed recently — its trailing return over one or more lookback windows.
  2. Rank and pick the handful of strongest performers (the "top-N").
  3. Weight the chosen funds.
  4. Rebalance on a fixed weekly-anchored schedule, repeating the cycle.

Selection is rank-only by default: the strategy holds the current leaders, with no requirement that a fund's momentum be positive. Even in a market where every fund has fallen, the default still allocates to the least-bad performers rather than automatically going to cash. An optional engulfing gate (selection.safe_asset_filter) overrides that by requiring each pick to beat the safe sleeve's own score by a margin; it is armed on the SOV and cushioned lineups and is itself a searchable axis (see GRID_SEARCH.md §2.5). The scoring recipe (for example, blending one-, three-, and six-month returns versus using a single twelve-month window) is a configurable choice; labels such as "ADM" or "DM" seen on the dashboard describe that recipe, not a filter.

How funds are weighted and how many are selected are also configurable; the default is equal weighting across the selected funds, the canonical Antonacci-style choice. The full menu of weighting and selection options is documented in GRID_SEARCH.md, and FEATURE_MATRIX.md records which live strategies use which.

1.1 The safe-asset defence (a concept)

The framework's primary defence against crashes comes from ranking, not from a filter. The asset catalog includes a low-volatility "safe" sleeve — a cash proxy that tracks the euro short-term rate (€STR). When a strategy includes this safe sleeve among its candidates, its small steady positive score naturally rises to the top of the ranking when equity scores collapse in a bear market, producing a defensive, mostly-cash allocation organically. A strategy that does not include the safe sleeve forgoes this defence and stays invested in the least-bad equity through downturns. In short: what a strategy lists among its candidates is what it can hold. The engulfing gate (selection.safe_asset_filter) adds an explicit second line on top of this, requiring each pick to clear the safe sleeve's score by a margin before it can be held.

1.2 Rotation versus buy-and-hold

Two kinds of strategy coexist on the dashboard:

  • Rotation strategies run the score → rank → weight → rebalance cycle described above.
  • Buy-and-hold strategies allocate once and never rebalance. They serve as static benchmarks — including the single-fund benchmarks that populate the regional and World pages — so that rotation results can be read against a do-nothing baseline.

2. Where the price history comes from

Most PEA-eligible UCITS ETFs in the universe only launched between 2014 and 2024, yet the backtest reaches back to 2008 to span the Global Financial Crisis. To get pre-launch history, each fund's series is stitched from older proxy data.

2.1 Proxy-chain stitching

Each fund can declare an ordered proxy chain — a list of older series, cleanest first — used to extend its history before the fund itself existed:

  1. Fetch the live ETF series (only from its real inception onward).
  2. Fetch the proxy series. Proxies may be quoted in euros directly, or in US dollars and converted to euros using the daily ECB EUR/USD reference rate.
  3. Rescale each proxy so its level matches the next, cleaner series on the handoff date. Pre-handoff levels are then walked back using the proxy's own returns.
  4. Concatenate: proxy data before inception, the real ETF afterward.

The result is a single continuous series with no artificial jump on the splice date. A multi-stage chain layers progressively older (and slightly less pure) proxies, each one only extending the segment before the cleaner data begins, so the recent, clean history is never contaminated by the deeper, dirtier backfill. The asset catalog (etfs-universe.yaml) records each fund's chain and splice dates.

2.2 Synthetic proxies

When no single older fund can cover a fund's pre-history, the chain can use a synthetic proxy: a small recipe that constructs an appropriate euro-denominated series from longer-history building blocks — for example, an EUR-hedged Japan or S&P 500 series reconstructed from a foreign-currency index plus the euro short-rate carry, or a 2×-leveraged variant reconstructed from its unleveraged underlying with the financing cost and fees applied. These synthetic segments are recorded with the same "stitched proxy" provenance as any other backfill.

2.3 Bad-data scrubbing

Public price feeds occasionally return spurious values — a price that drops by half and recovers the next day, or a frozen "flat run" that is really a forward-filled gap. The pipeline detects and removes these "round-trip" spikes and flat-run artefacts at two stages (on each individual series before stitching, and again on the full combined dataset), replacing the corrupt value so it does not distort returns. Genuinely implausible moves that are not round-trips are raised for human review rather than silently patched.

2.4 Survivorship and selection bias (an important caveat)

The universe is hand-curated from today's listings, and that embeds two biases that stitching cannot remove:

  • Survivorship bias. ETFs that closed, merged, or delisted between 2008 and today are simply absent — the catalog contains only funds that survived. A 2008 investor could not have known which would still exist in 2026.
  • Selection bias from hindsight proxies. Back-extending a fund before it existed uses proxies chosen with full knowledge of which indices turned out to matter; nobody back-extends the trackers of themes that died.

The framework accepts this rather than pretending to fix it, for a few reasons. The rotation signal is relative momentum across broad regional benchmark indices (MSCI World, the S&P 500, EM Asia, EuroStoxx, and the like), not single-stock or thematic picking — index families of this breadth existed throughout the window and none disappeared, so the bias is far milder than in stock-level backtests where dead names carry the loss tail. The buy-and-hold benchmarks share the same universe and the same stitching, so relative comparisons (rotation versus holding the same sleeves) are much less distorted than absolute levels. And the validation that the project leans on is out-of-sample, not the flattering full-sample curve.

Practical reading: treat absolute full-sample returns as upper-bound estimates, and put the most weight on out-of-sample results — especially the further back a backtest reaches past a fund's real inception. The store-of-value sleeves (gold, crypto) deserve the most caution, since they were added to the universe precisely because their recent decade looked the way it did.

3. The cost model

Trading is not free, and the backtest charges for it. Each time a strategy rebalances, the cost is computed per fund, on the amount of that fund that actually changes hands, not as a flat fee on total turnover. Two components are charged:

  • a broker commission — a small one-way percentage reflecting a typical French retail brokerage tier; and
  • half the bid-ask spread — the strategy is assumed to buy at the ask and sell at the bid, so half the round-trip spread is paid on each traded amount.

The cost is applied as a negative return on the day the trade fills. Per-fund spread estimates are kept as static figures in the asset catalog (etfs-universe.yaml), refreshed by hand as real-world spreads drift — public feeds do not provide reliable historical spreads, so a considered static estimate is the honest approach. Tighter-book flagship funds carry a few basis points; thin satellite and single-country sleeves carry considerably more.

4. Execution timing and warm-up

The backtest is careful never to use information it could not have had at the time.

  • Strategies rebalance on a Saturday-anchored schedule (the exact cadence — weekly, monthly, quarterly, and so on — is configurable). Two cadences sit outside that anchor and are described in §4.0: daily, which rebalances on every trading session, and the sub-daily every_Nh used by the 24/7 crypto panel. Saturday is the first calendar day after Friday's close, mirroring a real investor reviewing signals over the weekend.
  • The signal is computed from the preceding Friday's close.
  • The trade fills the following Monday, the first candle that opens after the decision, at the conservative prices of §4.0a. Costs are charged on that fill.

This ordering guarantees no look-ahead: the decision uses only data available before the trade.

4.0a Which candle is read for what — the price-usage doctrine

Two things read candles, and they read different ones (GitHub #64; the code is xmom/backtest/execution_price.py, whose docstring is the normative text).

  1. The signal — momentum and everything else that defines the new allocation at rebalance time T — may use only candles that are complete at T: the candle immediately prior to T and earlier, any component or combination of them. On the daily cadences that is Friday's candle for a Saturday rebalance; on the hourly path the rebalance time is the close of the signal bar, so the signal is that bar and the fill is the next one.
  2. The execution is priced off the candle that opens at T (the rebalance candle: Monday for a Saturday rebalance, the next bar on the hourly path), conservatively — a buy pays (open + high) / 2, a sell receives (open + low) / 2. The engine carries this as the open (the mark) plus a direction-dependent slippage on every traded unit, so a unit held through a rebalance pays nothing and a unit that changes hands pays exactly its half-range beside the fee and spread of §3.
  3. P&L and metrics therefore exist only once that candle is complete. A rebalance whose execution candle is not in the panel — the last bar of an hourly range on the grid, a Saturday whose Monday is past the data — books no fill, no cost and no P&L; the equity curve is read at the last complete candle, marked to its close, so the last rebalance with a booked fill is the one-but-last.

The single fill_mode knob (a function parameter, never a CLI flag) keeps one comparator: close marks at the close with no slippage — the optimistic fill a trader cannot count on — and exists for sensitivity only.

4.0 Where a backtest starts and stops

One rule for the start: the first rebalance is due at the FIRST CANDLE — the first session on a daily panel, the first bar on an hourly one — whatever the cadence. Every rebalance after it follows the cadence purely.

candles first rebalance then
daily the first session the cadence's own rule: weekly/biweekly at the next Saturday, monthly at the last Saturday of the first month, quarterly at its quarter end
hourly (every_Nh, crypto) the first bar the next grid-aligned bar

A backtest opening on Thursday 2024-02-01 therefore takes its position that Thursday under every cadence, and then resumes: weekly on 02-03, monthly on 02-24, quarterly on 04-27. The arms of a cadence axis all open on the same day and diverge only by cadence, which is what makes them comparable.

The opening rebalance uses session semantics — signal on that candle's own close, fill on the next candle — because there is no preceding Friday to read at the very start of the data. Every later rebalance of a Saturday-anchored cadence keeps the usual Friday-signal / Monday-fill triple described above.

There is no forced first Saturday. That rule applied until 2026-08-19 and put the opening rebalance on a calendar anchor rather than at the start of the data, leaving a strategy uninvested for up to a week (and up to N−1 hours on the hourly path) at the front of its own backtest.

The end is deliberately NOT synchronised with anything. Metrics are read at the last candle of the backtest range, whatever the cadence and whenever the last rebalance happened — the position is held and compounded past it rather than the curve being truncated there. A quarterly strategy whose final rebalance was weeks before the end is therefore measured over exactly the same window as a daily one, which is what makes the two comparable at all.

Strategies that include the safe sleeve can sit in cash during warm-up; strategies without one simply begin their plotted history when they first have a real signal.

4.1 The lookback reads pre-start history

A momentum score needs history before the day it is computed. The backtest therefore lets the scoring window read price history from before the backtest's nominal start date — the start date trims only the displayed equity and metric panel, not the price history feeding the score. A backtest that starts in 2008 is fully invested from day one, scoring funds off 2007 prices, rather than idling in cash while it warms up.

A data-history guard protects against a subtler trap: a fund whose history begins too late would be silently excluded from selection until it accumulated enough data. When a start date is set explicitly, the backtest refuses to run — naming the offending funds — if any candidate lacks enough history before the start to compute its longest lookback. The fix is to extend that fund's proxy backfill (see §2) or to move the start date later. A full-range backtest is exempt: it intentionally warms each fund in as soon as it has enough history.

5. Performance metrics

Each strategy's daily equity curve and rebalance log are summarised by a standard set of metrics, including:

  • CAGR — the compound annual growth rate over the run.
  • Maximum drawdown — the worst peak-to-trough decline.
  • Sharpe ratio — return per unit of total volatility.
  • Sortino ratio — like Sharpe, but penalising only downside volatility.
  • Hit rate — the fraction of intervals that ended higher than they began. Because a per-day hit rate is dominated by quiet drift days, the more meaningful figure is the per-rebalance hit rate: did equity at the next rebalance exceed equity at this one? That matches the actual bet the strategy makes each cycle.
  • plus turnover, total cost, drawdown-duration measures, and the final equity multiple.

By convention the risk-free rate is treated as zero in the Sharpe and Sortino ratios. The framework already absorbs the risk-free return at the strategy level — a strategy that holds the €STR safe sleeve earns that yield directly in its equity curve when it rotates to safety — so subtracting a risk-free rate again at the metric stage would double-count it. This keeps the ratios directly comparable across strategies that treat the safe sleeve the same way.

6. Discovery and redundancy

Beyond the active strategies, the broad PEA-eligible universe is also used for exploration: a correlation matrix across funds feeds a clustering step that groups funds whose daily returns move almost identically, and picks the lowest-fee member of each group as its representative. Because the fee is known in advance, that choice cannot be biased by hindsight performance. A diagnostic then flags strategies that hold two near-identical funds (keep one) or that hold a fund when a cheaper, equivalent one exists (replace it).

7. Glossary

A few terms used throughout the dashboard and these docs:

  • Momentum rotation — periodically reallocating to the assets with the strongest recent performance.
  • Rank-only selection — choosing assets purely by their score ranking, with no positive-momentum floor. The optional engulfing gate (selection.safe_asset_filter) layers a safe-asset margin on top.
  • Safe asset / safe sleeve — a low-volatility cash proxy tracking the euro short-term rate (€STR). When included among a strategy's candidates, it can win the top ranks during crashes and provide a defensive allocation.
  • Lookback — the trailing window over which a fund's recent return is measured to form its momentum score.
  • Rebalance / cadence — the act of recomputing and re-applying the allocation, and the fixed (Saturday-anchored) schedule on which it happens.
  • Proxy chain — the ordered set of older series used to extend a fund's price history back before its real inception.
  • Stitching — splicing a fund's real series onto its rescaled proxy history into one continuous level series.
  • Buy-and-hold benchmark — a static, never-rebalanced allocation used as a baseline for comparison.
  • PEA — the French Plan d'Épargne en Actions, the tax-advantaged equity account whose eligibility rules define this universe.

For the configurable strategy formalism behind these concepts, see GRID_SEARCH.md; for the mapping of features to strategies, FEATURE_MATRIX.md; and for the advanced methods, METHODOLOGY_ADVANCED.md.
